Reading the parameters

Each reading below comes with the evidence behind it: the 323 reference genomes (repeat content from EDTA for transposable elements and TRASH for satellites) and simulated 500-kb genomes where the repeat content is known. Open a panel to see the plot. Once you load a curve, your value is drawn as a black line.

k0_1 · first transition
The k at which most of the genome becomes unique. In random sequence this happens at k ≈ log4(genome size). Across the reference genomes k0_1 tracks log4(genome size) closely (r = ), about one k above it (median +).

L2 · second component
The share of k-mers that become unique only at the second, later transition: sequence in several copies that are similar but not identical (intermediate copy identity, e.g. young transposable elements, partly homogenised arrays). Across the reference genomes L2 tracks transposable-element content (r = ), not satellite content (r = ). It is not total repeat content: in simulation, copies that differ by 15% or more become unique together with the rest of the genome and add little to L2. If the curve has no clear second transition (k0_2 close to k0_1), the split between L1 and L2 has no meaning.
Show the evidence

Left: L2 against transposable-element content in the reference genomes. Right: simulated genomes, each with 30% of its sequence in 5-kb repeat copies, differing only in how far the copies have diverged. The repeat amount is constant, but L2 falls as the copies diverge. At 1%, some copies are still not unique at k = 151 and count against the asymptote instead. Open circle: the second transition has merged with the first (k0_2 within 2 of k0_1), so L2 there means nothing.

k0_2 · second transition
The k at which those similar copies become distinguishable: a k-mer must be long enough to include a difference between copies. In simulation k0_2 falls as copies diverge (), so less-diverged, typically younger, copies give a larger k0_2. In a real genome k0_2 averages over many repeat families of different ages. It has not yet been tested against an independent measure of repeat age. Across the reference genomes it is unrelated to transposable-element content (r = ) and to genome size (r = ).

asymptote = L1 + L2
Fraction unique at the longest k. The rest, 1 − asymptote, is sequence in copies so similar that k = 151 still cannot tell them apart. In simulation, 1 − asymptote equals the share of the genome in perfect tandem arrays. In real genomes it correlates only moderately with satellite content (r = ) and with transposable-element content (r = ). It reflects the most homogeneous copies rather than the total amount of repeats: in the human genome, rDNA and α-satellite higher-order arrays never become unique, while other satellite arrays do. It can only count copies that are present in the assembly.

Left: simulated genomes with 2–50% of their sequence in perfect tandem arrays; the dashed line is equality. Right: 1 − asymptote against satellite content in the reference genomes.

s1, s2 · steepness
How sharp each transition is. In random sequence the rise is steep (slope ≈ in simulation). In real genomes s2 shows no simple relation with transposable-element content (r = ). Read s1 and s2 as describing the shape of the curve, not as measures of a particular repeat type.
